Dancing levitated diamonds in a modified Paul trap

The experiment

Paul traps use oscillating electric fields to confine charged particles. In this experiment, microscopic diamonds were charged near a high-voltage needle and loaded into a modified trap. They were initially stable, but over several hours their motion became larger and more erratic before they eventually escaped.

The behaviour was first noticed while the apparatus was part of a public installation at Science Gallery London. My project focused on measuring the change in motion and identifying a plausible physical cause, while working with difficult particle loading, high-voltage electrodes and a non-standard trap geometry.

Tracking the particles

I developed MATLAB software to follow the particles frame by frame, repeatedly recentering a small analysis window so that fast motion did not cause the tracker to lose them. The reconstructed trajectories showed tight confinement shortly after loading and much wider, slowly rotating motion around five hours later—the “dancing” behaviour that gave the project its name.

A possible loss of charge

The power spectrum of the motion shifted to a slightly lower frequency as the confinement weakened. A simple Paul-trap simulation reproduced that direction of change when the particle’s charge-to-mass ratio was reduced.

Gradual charge loss is therefore a plausible explanation: a less strongly charged particle couples more weakly to the trapping field. Because charge was not measured directly, this remains an interpretation rather than a conclusive identification.

This work was carried out through a King’s Undergraduate Research Fellowship with Professor James Millen in the Levitated Nanophysics Group.

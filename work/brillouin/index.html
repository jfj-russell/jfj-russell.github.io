Multimode Brillouin scattering and its temperature dependence in optical fibre

The spectrum

Stimulated Brillouin scattering is an interaction between light and sound in an optical fibre. It is often described by a single resonance near 11 GHz, but the fibre can support several acoustic modes with closely spaced frequencies. In this project, I investigated that multimode structure and asked whether the separation between the modes could provide a more robust observable than their absolute frequency.

Using heterodyne spectroscopy in a long length of standard SMF-28 fibre, we resolved five neighbouring peaks. Four are consistent with guided longitudinal acoustic modes, while the fifth is better interpreted as a cladding resonance. Their separations are approximately 76–82 MHz.

Experiment

A narrow-linewidth pump was sent through about 10 km of fibre. The backscattered light was mixed with a local oscillator and measured on a spectrum analyser, allowing several overlapping frequency windows to be combined into one spectrum. I fitted the peaks with Lorentzian lineshapes and focused on their relative spacing, which is less sensitive than the absolute frequency to common calibration offsets.

Acoustic-mode model

I developed an axisymmetric finite-element model of the fibre. A gradual change in acoustic velocity across the doped core supports four guided modes and naturally produces their near-even spacing; a sharp step-index model does not reproduce the measurement.

A simpler scalar model gives the leading temperature-dependent mode frequency as

fm(T) = qB(T)V1(T) 2π [ 1+ 2δ(T) qB(T)Rg(T) (2m−1) ]

where the Brillouin wavevector is defined as:

qB(T) = 4πneff(T) λp

The graded core behaves approximately like a harmonic potential for sound, producing a sequence of nearly equally spaced modes. Small departures from this limit account for the remaining variation in the measured spacings.

Temperature dependence

The absolute Brillouin frequencies mainly follow the temperature-dependent sound speed of silica. Much of this common shift cancels when two neighbouring modes are compared, leaving a smaller change that is more sensitive to the difference between the core and cladding.

Across the full modelled temperature range, the individual resonances move by about 260 MHz while their spacings change by only about 4 MHz. This suppression suggests that mode spacing could be useful when common frequency offsets or calibration drift need to be rejected, although the prediction still requires direct experimental testing.

This work was supervised by Dr Michael Vanner in the Quantum Measurement Lab. James Rose contributed to the numerical modelling through discussions and support with NumBAT.

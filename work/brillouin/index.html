Multimode Brillouin scattering and its temperature dependence in optical fibre

The spectrum

Stimulated Brillouin scattering arises from the interaction of light with acoustic waves in an optical fibre. Although it is often described by a single resonance near 11 GHz, several closely spaced resonances can arise from different acoustic modes supported by the fibre.

My project investigated how the spacing between these resonances changes with temperature. I first measured the multimode spectrum of standard SMF-28 fibre, resolving five neighbouring peaks separated by approximately 76–82 MHz. These measurements provided a basis for modelling the acoustic modes and predicting their temperature dependence.

Experiment

A narrow-linewidth pump was sent through about 10 km of fibre. The backscattered light was mixed with a local oscillator and measured on a spectrum analyser, allowing several overlapping frequency windows to be combined into one spectrum. I fitted the peaks with Lorentzian lineshapes and focused on their relative spacing, which is less sensitive than the absolute frequency to common calibration offsets.

Modelling the acoustic modes

To understand the measured mode spacing, I developed a simplified model of acoustic confinement in the fibre. The germania-doped core has a lower acoustic velocity than the surrounding silica, allowing it to guide several acoustic modes with different radial profiles.

Modelling the core with a gradually varying acoustic velocity produces an approximately equally spaced sequence of resonances, consistent with the measured spectrum. The model relates their spacing to the acoustic properties and effective guiding radius of the fibre, providing a basis for predicting how the resonances respond to temperature.

Temperature dependence

I extended the acoustic model using temperature-dependent material properties to predict how the individual resonances and their spacings change with temperature. Although the absolute frequencies respond strongly to changes in the acoustic velocity of silica, neighbouring modes share much of this response, so their relative spacing changes considerably less.

Across the modelled temperature range, the resonances undergo a common frequency shift of approximately 260 MHz, while neighbouring-mode spacings change by only around 4 MHz. The remaining response depends on differences between the thermo-acoustic properties of the doped core and surrounding silica.

This suggests that mode spacing could provide a way to probe changes in acoustic confinement while suppressing much of the common frequency shift. The predicted temperature dependence has not yet been measured experimentally.

This work was supervised by Dr Michael Vanner in the Quantum Measurement Lab. James Rose contributed to the numerical modelling through discussions and support with NumBAT.
